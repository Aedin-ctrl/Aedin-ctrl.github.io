Before you act on one of these

Filling both legs does not make you safe here. On a true hedge exactly one leg pays and the total is fixed. These pairings are not that: they pay $1 when the two venues agree, and $2 or $0 when they do not. The downside is real and it is why the movement filter exists. Settlement is at most fifteen minutes away, so there is no holding period to speak of.

Between the two fills you are not safe, and that is where the money is actually lost. There is no atomic order across two venues: you place one, then the other. Both quote in whole cents, so a single tick is 1% of the payout — larger than most of the edges on this board. If the second venue moves one cent while you are getting there, you either hold one naked leg — an outright directional bet on the coin — or you complete at a worse price and lose more than the gap was worth.

These rows are short-lived by construction — each window lives fifteen minutes and Kalshi lists one at a time. More to the point, about four in five candidates lose their edge when both legs are re-read together a few seconds later. Measured drift on the re-read averages −0.13¢, so it is not a one-way leak; it is that the cheapest quote in a scan is the most likely to be the stale one. None of this is priced into the figures above — they assume both legs fill at the quoted price, instantly.

Reading a card

This is not an arbitrage. Kalshi and Polymarket both run “will it go up” markets on the same 15-minute clock, but they resolve different questions. Kalshi compares the average of the last 60 seconds to the average of the 60 seconds before the open. Polymarket compares a time-weighted average of the whole window to the price at the open. A coin that sits flat for 14 minutes and spikes in the last one is up on Kalshi and down on Polymarket.

So buying the cheap side on each venue pays $1 when the two agree, and $2 or $0 on the two kinds of disagreement. Over 34,326 windows those two kinds came out nearly balanced (6.08% against 5.26%), which is why the price gap between the venues is the thing worth having rather than a premium for taking a risk. The big number on each card is that gap after fees, per contract.

A positive gap is not enough on its own. Kalshi's fee is 0.07 × price × (1 − price) per contract, biggest in the middle of the range — 1.75¢ at a coin flip against 0.33¢ at 5¢ or 95¢. The board has measured edges of a fifth of a cent to nine cents a contract, so the fee is routinely larger than the edge, and a pairing only clears when it is priced well away from the middle.

Disagreement is the risk, and it is predictable. It collapses as the window moves: 40.9% of windows that moved under 1bp disagreed, against 0.4% of those that moved more than 40bp. Pairings on a window that has barely moved are held back for that reason, not because the price is wrong. Real settlements so far: 98.6% agreement — but that rests on a handful of disagreements, so treat it as thin evidence rather than a rate.
