Trade history

Reading the board

One column per venue. Each card is a live 15-minute window on that venue, in that venue's own wording, with that venue's own line, the time left on the clock, and what each side costs right now. Both columns are priced from the same streamed order books the trader decides on. The check or cross on a card is real money — whether an actual position is open on that exact market, read from the venue's own portfolio endpoint, with the amount.

Kalshi and Polymarket US are the same contract. Both settle on the same CF Benchmarks index, by the same rule: the simple average of the sixty seconds before the close against the simple average of the sixty seconds before the open. Over 356 settled windows the two venues published the same strike to the cent on BTC, ETH and SOL. (This is not true of global Polymarket, which uses a Chainlink time-weighted average against the opening price. That is a different contract, and it is not what this board shows.)

So buying the cheap side on each venue pays $1 when the two agree, and $2 or $0 when they disagree. Over 384 settled windows on these two contracts they agreed 383 times — 99.74%. The single disagreement was XRP. That residual is the real risk and it does not shrink with the fee, but one event is one event: the true rate could plausibly be several times higher.

Correction. This page previously said 98.25% over 1,256 windows. That figure was measured on global Polymarket — a different contract, on a venue these accounts cannot reach, and its sample even included coins Polymarket US does not list. It was the wrong number for the thing being traded.

Why the number next to each pairing is negative

The price on a card is the top of the book — what the first contract costs. Twenty contracts cost more, because the ladder has to be walked, and Polymarket US is thin at the touch: a median of 67 contracts against Kalshi's 328. The pairing figures are quoted at the size actually shown, which is why a card can look cheap while nothing clears.

The fee is quadratic. Kalshi charges 0.07 × price × (1 − price) per contract and Polymarket US 0.0695 of the same shape, so the round trip is 3.8¢ at a coin flip and 0.30¢ out at 3¢. But the fee turns out not to be what stops this.

Correction. This page previously said the venues' mids differ by “0.00¢ in every band” and that the whole loss was the fee. That was an artifact. The sampler writes both pairings off the same two mid prices, and the two are exact negatives of each other, so pooling them forced the median to zero by construction — it would have printed 0.00¢ if the venues had disagreed by a nickel all day. 81,000 samples bought nothing; eight would have said the same.

What actually stops it is the spread, not the fee. In the cheapest band the round trip costs 0.30¢ but the two bid–ask spreads cost about 0.55¢ — and both venues sit at exactly one tick, the narrowest price either can quote. You cannot cross two spreads to collect a gap whose median is 0.35¢. Taker-to-taker in that band is −0.99¢ per contract, and that number is a real measurement rather than an artifact. Polymarket US quotes on a flat 1¢ grid while Kalshi quotes to 0.1¢ in the tails, so the coarser grid alone is wider than the fee it is supposed to beat.

Two things remain genuinely open rather than closed. The maker route was reported here as closed; that measurement has been withdrawn — its fill proxy counted a fill whenever the price ticked down, so it selected the adverse half of the sample and then measured how adverse it was. Running it on the rows where the price ticked up gives the exact mirror image, which means the test had no power at all. And Massachusetts does not adopt the federal wagering-loss rule, so a hedged pair may be taxed on its gross winnings — roughly 2.5¢ against a 3¢ edge — though whether these are instead §1256 contracts, which would void that entirely, is unresolved.
