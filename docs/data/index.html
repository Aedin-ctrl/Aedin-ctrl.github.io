Every crypto up/down window on Kalshi, Polymarket and Gemini — the whole path of each one, recorded every few seconds, and how it actually settled. Riding on top: a paper test of one rule, with $100 of play money per venue. No real money is involved anywhere in this page.

The rule being tested

Wait until the last fifth of the window — minute 4 of 5, minute 12 of 15. Look at which way the coin is moving right then. Buy that side, cross the spread, pay the venue's real fee, hold to settlement. One contract a time.

What it actually buys, which was not what I expected. The prediction here was that the rule would pay 80–90¢ for the side already winning. It does the opposite: in the first hour, 22 of 23 trades bought the underdog, at an average of 24¢. Because "trending" means the coin's direction of travel and not "is it above the window's open", the rule mostly fires when the price rose early and is now drifting back — so it buys the cheap side, betting the drift continues. That is a bet on short-term reversal against the window's position, which is a different and more interesting thing than backing the favourite.

It still has to clear a real cost: the spread on these venues gets wider near the close, not tighter, and fills here cross it and pay the venue's fee. Prices below 2¢ and above 97¢ are refused — at those ends the market is saying the window is already over, and a 0.1¢ contract that happens to win pays a thousandfold and would swamp every other trade in the record.

Each account, broken down — filter it any way you like

There is one account per venue per timeframe, $100 each. A 5-minute window and an hourly one are different bets with different spreads and different amounts of noise to cut through, so averaging them into one number answers neither question. Splitting by coin as well would be seventy-odd accounts of a handful of trades each, so coins stay as a filter inside an account rather than as accounts of their own.

Two further accounts now run beside the original rule rather than replacing it, so its record stays continuous:

  • fade_e450_l360_k20 — promoted by the sweep's own gate at p = 0.003. With seven and a half minutes left on a 15-minute window, and the price still within 20 basis points of where it opened, it buys the side the last six minutes moved against. It is right about half the time and pays about 41¢ for it, so the money comes from the price, not from being right. It trades every coin and every venue.
  • basket5 — the same rule on the five coins that made money in both halves of the record (bnb, btc, xrp, eth, doge). This one did not earn its place. Running the identical coin-picking procedure on a coin-flip world beats it 7.6% of the time, so the coins were chosen by hindsight and the basket is here only to be settled forward. It is marked a challenger, not a promotion.

Both were chosen on roughly one day of recorded windows. That is the real caveat, and no control can fix it — only more days can.

ROI is profit over what was actually risked — the honest denominator for a contract bought at 85¢. Hit is how often the side bought was the side that won.

The sweep — every rule variant, replayed against the record

The accounts above test one rule, chosen by guesswork. This replays the same recorded windows against thousands of variants: when to jump in, how far back to look, follow or fade, and how much the price's distance from the open should matter. It trades nothing — it prices each variant against the real book that was quoted at the time.

Read the control column, not the profit column. A grid of thousands produces a spectacular winner from pure noise — that is what a grid does. So the whole grid is re-scored against a no-signal world and the best of that is recorded. If the no-signal world wins as often as this one, there is nothing here. Nothing is promoted to a live account unless that test passes.

The no-signal world is built by flipping the side, not by shuffling the outcomes. Shuffling outcomes while leaving prices alone breaks the link between the two — a 10¢ ticket starts winning half the time — so the no-signal world comes out wildly profitable and every rule looks worthless against it. That is what this sweep did for its first several days, and it reads exactly like "there is no edge". The honest null leaves the market untouched and randomises only the thing the rule claims to know: which side to buy, at the same instant, paying the same book.

Why trades were not taken — the denominator

A strategy that logs only its fills reports the average of the trades it was able to get, which is a different and much more flattering number. Every refusal is recorded with its reason.

These are not failed paper fills. A paper account could fill everything — that is exactly what would make it useless. No offer on that side means there was no ask resting on the book at all, so filling would be inventing liquidity that did not exist. Price below 0.02 / above 0.97 are this project's own bounds: at those ends the market is saying the window is over. No signal is different again — the coin barely moved, so the rule had no view and never tried. It is counted separately from the buy rate on the cards above for that reason.

Can you even buy it? — offers by how far through the window

How often each side has an offer resting on it, by tenth of the window (up / down, as percentages). Offers thin out sharply towards the close, and once a window is effectively decided — the favourite bid at 99¢ — its ask disappears completely: you can buy the loser for a penny, but the side the rule wants cannot be bought at any price. Measured at 73 seconds left on a window that was not yet decided, the favourite still had 509 contracts offered at 97¢, so this is about the decided case specifically, not a blanket rule. Either way the rule is unbuyable exactly when it is most confident, which is the refusal that shows up most often below.

What exists to trade — checked against all three venues

No venue runs an hourly or daily up/down market any more. Kalshi and Gemini list strike ladders at those lengths ("BTC $64,400 or above" — 10 rungs on Gemini, up to 188 on Kalshi); Polymarket's hourly and daily up/down windows were discontinued after 2025.

A ladder becomes the same bet by taking the rung nearest the price at the window's open: "above that strike at the close" is then "finished higher than it started". That is how the hourly and daily accounts trade. It is an approximation — the strike is a round number and the opening price is not — so how far off centre each one sits is recorded per window in basis points, and anything more than 60 bps off is refused rather than traded as if the question had not changed. On a $100 grid at a $64,800 coin the offset is at most ~7.7 bps.

Weekly and monthly ladders are listed by the venues and are deliberately not traded: a window measured in weeks cannot be tested by a rule about its last few minutes inside any sensible run.

Paper only — there is no order path in this system and no venue credential with write scope. Collected on the Pi every 15 seconds.
