What this test does and does not model

Data. Daily bars from the Yahoo chart API, split and dividend adjusted, 2011 to today (3,770 trading days). The open is adjusted by the same ratio as the close, so splits do not appear as overnight returns. Dividend adjustment is multiplicative and therefore preserves returns exactly, so a backward-adjusted series is the right input for a total-return strategy.

How much of it gets used. All of it, but not all of it as a verdict. At the default 50% in-sample share, the first half is training only and the out-of-sample result covers roughly the last 7.5 years — which includes the 2020 crash and the 2022 bear market. Lowering the in-sample share widens the out-of-sample period at the cost of shorter training windows.

Modelled: turnover cost per trade, financing on exposure above 1x, borrow on short exposure, an optional per-period holding drag, an exposure cap, and an execution stress sweep covering higher costs, missed trades and variable slippage.

Not modelled, and these matter:

  • Market impact. Fine at retail size, wrong at scale. Nothing here tells you capacity.
  • Intraday execution. Positions multiply the full close-to-close return, which assumes you trade at the close. Realistic for market-on-close orders; not for anything that needs a specific intraday price, and a stop-loss cannot be modelled at all on daily bars.
  • Survivorship. These are index ETFs, which survived — but so did the index, and an index drops its failures by construction. That bias is one a real holder also faces, so it is not a backtest artefact here. It would be, badly, for individual stocks.
  • Tax. Zero throughout. The Monte Carlo mode models it; this does not.
  • Regime change. Fifteen years is one history, not a sample of possible histories. The Monte Carlo stage is the partial answer to that, and it is partial.

The honest summary: this is a realistic test of a daily, close-to-close, retail-sized rule on a liquid ETF. It is not a realistic test of anything intraday, anything leveraged beyond what a broker will finance, or anything that needs a specific fill price.

How this works, and what it cannot tell you

What it does. It takes a return series — yours, or one generated by a model fitted to yours — and replays it thousands of times, applying your sizing, costs and tax rules to each path. Every path is compounded with an absorbing floor: an account that reaches zero stays there rather than multiplying back through a negative.

Reproducibility. Path p always draws from random substream p, derived from the seed alone. The result is identical whether it runs on one core or sixteen, today or next year. The run fingerprint under the export button covers every input.

What it cannot do. A simulation cannot create information that is not in your data. If you resample a backtest, you learn how much of its shape was ordering luck — not whether the edge was real, because the bootstrap inherits whatever edge the sample happened to contain. That is what Part 2 is for, and why it is deliberately hostile. A model fitted to a quiet sample will simulate quiet futures; the worst event in your simulation is usually bounded by the worst event in your data, which is precisely the event you should worry about.

The number that matters most is rarely the median. It is the fifth percentile, the drawdown you would have had to sit through, and the probability of ruin at the leverage you actually intend to use.
